# Lettr design direction

This is the owner's design taste for Lettr marketing work, not a critique of every current component. Use it with the product context and live visual system. The goal is **fewer simultaneous ideas so the remaining ideas can be more expressive, memorable, interactive, and confident**. Simple should not become bland.

## Compose from the message

Decide in this order: **message → hierarchy → visual concept → interaction → composition → components**. Give a section one thing to remember and one obvious focal point. Use short copy and generous, intentional space. Let the heading, product moment, and supporting detail have visibly different weight. Large type is welcome when it sharpens the idea; asymmetry is welcome when it directs attention. Product UI can carry the narrative, but crop or simplify it until the important action is readable.

Prefer progressive disclosure when a product is complex: toggles, tabs, sliders, carousels, or a short interactive sequence can show modes or stages without showing every control at once. Interaction must make the underlying product relationship easier to understand; it must remain usable by keyboard, on touch, and with reduced motion. Use subtle animation to clarify change or continuity rather than to decorate a static pile of content.

For independent feature stories, the owner favors **large, spacious cards**: one confident heading, one graphic that explains that exact point, and a short line of support. Give each point enough room to feel memorable. A small number of these cards can be stronger than a dense feature inventory. Reinterpret references using Lettr's fonts, ink/blush/pink palette, product vocabulary, and square geometry; keep graphics legible and distinct from one another.

For the homepage, keep section headings smaller than the hero and use the hero's medium heading weight. When a heading has a pink phrase, begin that phrase on its own line. Keep a dark section dark through its cards and inner graphics; use Lettr's dark surface levels and pink accent rather than alternating light cards inside it.

## Avoid during concepting and critique

- Clutter, dense sections, excessive explanation, and several items competing at the same visual weight.
- Tiny labels, repeated all-caps mono eyebrows, badge/pill noise, and long paragraphs that a visual or interaction could replace.
- Tiny unreadable “product UI,” large screenshots with no focal area, complicated pseudo-dashboards, or decorative controls that tell no story.
- Dense card piles, generic bento grids, repeated `eyebrow + heading + paragraph + cards`, and section rhythms that never change. Large cards with one clear idea each are welcome when they make the story more expressive.
- Generic gradient SaaS styling, arbitrary new colors, fashionable rounded geometry, and reference styles pasted over Lettr's actual brand.

Before accepting a section, ask: **What can be removed without weakening the message? What single thing should someone remember?** If the second answer is unclear, simplify or rethink the section.

## Translating external inspiration

Study the reason a reference works: its information hierarchy, focal device, reveal, pacing, crop, or interaction. Borrow that principle and reinterpret it with Lettr's fonts, ink/blush/pink palette, square geometry, actual product vocabulary, and relevant UI. Do not average multiple references into a generic middle. A strong dominant direction can have smaller secondary influences. Record useful research in [`reference-notes.md`](reference-notes.md); the workflow skill explains when and how to search.
